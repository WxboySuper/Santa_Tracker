# World content editorial guide

Write for curious adults and families. Keep the voice warm and specific, with the occasional dry aside. Do not address readers as toddlers, turn cultures into costumes, or flatten a place into a Christmas postcard.

## Start with the place and the person

Describe a practice as something some people, communities, or households do. Name the place and group when the source supports that level of detail. Avoid implying that a whole country shares one belief or custom.

Good: "In parts of Mexico, some families hold Las Posadas gatherings in the days before Christmas. The details vary by community and household."

Avoid: "Mexico celebrates Christmas with Las Posadas." It treats a varied country as one household.

Use "Christmas" for the holiday and "winter" for the season when it is winter. Christmas is not the name of a season everywhere. Avoid calling December cold, snowy, or dark without checking the local climate and hemisphere.

## Places with different calendars or no Christmas tradition

Do not force a Christmas custom into every stop. A good card can explain a local landscape, language, food, animal, astronomy fact, public holiday, or seasonal event. If Christmas is a minority or absent tradition in the place, say so only when a reliable source establishes it and the fact adds context. Do not present absence as a curiosity or imply that residents are missing something.

Example: "Christmas is a Christian holiday, and it is not a major public or religious observance for everyone in this region. This stop is about the coastal ecology instead."

## Contested, changing, and sensitive claims

Separate established fact from interpretation. Attribute contested origin stories, disputed borders, political claims, and religious interpretations to named sources. Include the date or period the claim describes. Avoid declaring a single inventor, oldest example, or universal practice when sources disagree.

For Indigenous communities, use the community's preferred name and, where appropriate, its own cultural or educational source. Do not reuse sacred or restricted knowledge as decoration. For contested place names, follow the source approved by the editorial owner and explain the naming choice if visitors could reasonably be confused.

## Sources and attribution

Every factual card needs at least one source URL and a short source label in the content record. Prefer primary material: local museums, cultural institutions, national statistical or meteorological services, universities, community organizations, and the people or institutions responsible for a tradition. Use a reputable secondary source when primary material is unavailable. Search snippets and unattributed social posts are not sources.

Record the claim supported by each source, publication or update date when available, access/review date, author/reviewer, and any reuse or image licence. A source that supports a broad overview may not support an exact date, origin, or country-wide claim. Image rights need their own record; citing the page where an image appears does not grant permission to reuse it.

Keep claims short enough that the source can be checked. Link sources close to the relevant claim when the publishing format permits it. Do not copy long source passages; paraphrase and preserve the meaning.

## Review and staleness

Review each claim before publication and at least once per season. Recheck sooner if the claim concerns current borders, population, weather records, public policy, a living person, or a changing event. Mark a claim stale when its source is unavailable, its date is unclear for a time-sensitive statement, or newer evidence may change the wording. Remove, rewrite, or replace stale claims before publication. Do not quietly change an archived claim without recording the revision.

When a fact cannot be verified, leave it out or label the uncertainty plainly. A charming detail is not a reason to guess.
